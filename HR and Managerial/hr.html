2. Seven answer frames (memorise the shape, not the words)

Tell me about yourself

Present → Past → Future, 90 seconds. Present: who you are now. Past: 2–3 steps that matter (ranks, projects). Future: why this role. Ends at MathWorks.

STAR + Learning

Situation (1 line) → Task → Action (what I did, 60% of the answer) → Result (a number) → Learning + one-line MathWorks hook.

Why ladder (decisions)

For every decision on your timeline ask “why?” three times. If the third answer is weak, rewrite it. Reasons should be about what you wanted to learn or build, never “I didn't get X”.

Weakness

Real weakness → how it hurt once → what you do about it → proof it's improving. They ask for two (Anubhav insisted). Not “perfectionist”.

Feedback

What was said → how you felt/reacted → what you changed → what improved. Never argue with the feedback in the story.

Conflict

Idea vs idea, not person vs person: facts, talk privately, test or measure, agree, keep the relationship. Matches the value “encourage conflict of ideas, discourage conflict between people”.

Pressure / deadline / many tasks

List → rank by deadline and impact → protect the core → tell people early → deliver → what you'd do differently. A calm, logical order is what they want (your file: “explain how you would handle it logically and calmly”).

6. Cross-question drill (do this out loud)

  • After each answer, ask yourself: “Why?”, “Give an example”, “What exactly did YOU do?”, “What would you do differently?”. If you can't answer, the story is not ready.
  • Keep every answer under 2 minutes and end it with a result (the Codinzhub guide gives the same advice). Record yourself once on your phone and listen for filler words and pauses.
  • Ask a friend to play HR for 30 minutes (the real round is 30 minutes) using the groups above. Score: no generic answer, under 2 minutes, a number in every result, no long pauses, consistent facts.

How to do well (general GD practice, not from MathWorks)

  • Speak in the first 2–3 minutes with a short structure: “Let's look at it from three sides: X, Y and Z.” Being structured matters more than being first.
  • Make 2–3 points, each with one fact or example. Quality over airtime.
  • Build on others (“Adding to what she said …”), disagree with the idea and not the person, and invite a quiet member in once. That is MathWorks' “conflict of ideas, not between people” in action, and it shows leadership and calm.
  • Never interrupt, shout or dominate. If nobody summarises at the end, offer a balanced 20-second summary.
  • For pros-and-cons topics, give both sides and a reasoned conclusion.
Prepare this one topic properly: “Pros and cons of the EDG program”

Pros (official facts): you explore projects and teams before choosing; technical training and mentoring; a customer experience curriculum, so you learn how customers use the products; a leadership-development track. Cons / trade-offs (honest reasoning, not official): no fixed team at the start, so you must be self-driven and comfortable with ambiguity; ramping up on more than one codebase takes time; you specialise a little later than in a fixed-team role. Balanced close: “For someone who hasn't yet found their area, the exploration and mentoring are worth the slower start.” The same points answer “Any drawbacks of EDG?” in the managerial round.

Other topics to practise for 5 minutes each: electric vehicles (boon or bane), climate change and technology, social media and society, AI tools at work (MathWorks’ job description mentions agentic-AI tools).
